S1. Computational details

S1.1 Atomistic classical simulations

The crystallographic structure for the fully-metallated, reduced, wild-type human SOD1, resolved
at 1.09 Å by Strange et al1 (PDB code: 2C9V) was employed as a starting point. For residues with
more than one crystallographic conformation, the one with the highest occupancy was selected.
For the mutant structures, the G93A mutation was generated with PyMOL. The monomeric
structure was protonated with the Reduce program.2 The protein structures were treated with the
AMBER99SB-ILDN force field3,4 and the metal-site parameters for SOD1 obtained by Mera-
Adasme et al5. The structure was solvated on a TIP36 solvent box such that every protein atom
was at least 1 nm away from the side of the box. Na+ and Cl- ions were added to neutralization
plus a 100 mM NaCl concentration. Periodic boundary conditions were employed with the particle-
mesh Ewald method7,8 for the treating of long-range electrostatic interactions. The systems were
optimized and equilibrated for at least 1 ns in the NVT ensemble and 1 ns in the NPT ensemble.
3 production runs of 1 𝜇s each were performed in the NPT ensemble with the Nose-Hoover
thermostat9,10 and the Parrinello-Raman barostat11,12 with a reference temperature and pressure
of 310 K and 1 bar, respectively. The Gromacs program package 13,14 was employed for all
atomistic MD simulations.

Principal component analysis was performed on the resulting trajectories. The 3 widest
eigenvectors of the covariance matrix were projected onto the respective trajectory and analyzed
visually with PyMOL. Additional analyses (RMSD, RMSF, distances, hydration, among others)
were performed with Gromacs13 and with tools programmed ad-hoc by us.

S1.2 Hybrid quantum-mechanics / molecular mechanics (QM/MM) simulations.

The systems for QM/MM calculations were built from snapshots with the electrostatic loop in open
and closed conformations. Both structures were selected from the same G93A-SOD1 atomistic
trajectory. The protein was re-solvated and the quantum-chemical subsystem was defined to
properly treat the metal-site of SOD1. It included 590 atoms, 5 sub-sequences of the protein and
(25 residues) and 64 solvent molecules (Figure S1). Thus, it the QM site follows our previous
recommendations on QM size for QM/MM studies on SOD1. The system was optimized and
equilibrated to 310 K in spherical boundary conditions, with a sphere of 36 Å radius (Figure S2),
and a constraint for all atoms with an equilibrium position range covering all the 36 Å sphere, and
a force constant of 10 kJ/mol*Å. The HF-3c composite method from Grimme15, previously tested
on the system16, was employed for the QM part of the system, while the AMBER-ILDN force field3,4
was employed for the MM subsystem. Langevin dynamics production simulations were performed
in the NVT ensemble for 5.15 ps, where the first 1.15 ps were not considered for the analysis.
Four equilibration+simulations were performed: Electrostatic-loop open + Zn(II), Electrostatic-loop
closed + Zn(II), Electrostatic-loop open and Electrostatic loop closed. We obtained the average
potential energy (i.e. the full QM/MM energy) for each simulation (400 points for each simulation).
The four averaged values were added and subtracted according to Equation S1, to obtain the
difference in Zn(II)-binding enthalpy between the open electrostatic loop and the closed
electrostatic loop conformations. In Equation S1, we compare the Zn(II) binding to SOD1 with the
EL open and with the loop closed, obtaining a ΔΔH value which is not associated to interaction
energy for ion zinc of either conformation, but to the difference in interaction energy between both.
QM/MM calculations were performed with pDynamo17 , ORCA 4.018 and Turbomole v7.319,20.

An estimation of the ΔΔS for the process was obtained from a pure-QM central-difference,
numerical frequencies calculations on the full protein plus the water molecules within ~10 Å from
the metal center (34 water molecules) to a total of ~2300 atoms per system. Each system was
taken from the equilibrated corresponding structure and optimized in vacuo. Both the optimization
and the Hessian determination performed with the novel non self-consistent semiempirical
method by Grimme, GFN0-xTB21, which is optimized for both properties (geometries and
frequencies, in addition to non-covalent interactions).

S1.3 Coarse-grained (CG) simulations

All CG simulations were performed with the program package Gromacs13 (version 2018.1) and
the open-beta version of the Martini 3 force field22. The simulation parameters were chosen in
accordance to reference23. The leap-frog propagator was employed in combination with the Verlet
cutoff scheme and a buffer tolerance of 0.005 kJ/mol. Van-der-Waals interactions were treated
with the cutoff scheme using a cutoff distance of 1.1 nm; Coulomb interactions were treated with
the reaction-field method, a cutoff of 1.1 nm, and a dielectric constant of 𝜀r = 15 for water. The
temperature and pressure were controlled using a velocity-rescale thermostat (reference
temperature T = 310 K, coupling constant 𝜏T = 1 ps) and a Parrinello-Rahman barostat (p = 1 bar,
𝜏p = 12 ps, compressibility 𝛽 = 3⋅104 bar−1), respectively.

The protein was simulated in a cubic box with an edge length of 8.3 nm and solvated with
approximately 4,600 CG water beads which correspond to 18,400 water molecules. After the
protein net charge of −3 was neutralized, 145 mM NaCl was added. Each replica was minimized
for 5000 steps using a steepest descent algorithm, followed by an equilibration for 500 ps (time
step 𝛥t = 5 fs). Before the equilibration, random velocities were assigned to all particles
corresponding to a temperature of 310 K. Each of the twelve production runs was performed for
40 µs using a time step of 𝛥t = 20 fs.

The Martini protein model of SOD1 was generated from an equilibrated atomistic structure of the
wild-type enzyme with a closed electrostatic loop. In addition to the Martini protein model24, the
recently proposed sidechain corrections were used25. Following the procedure of Poma et al.26, a
Gō-like model was set up to maintain the tertiary structure of the protein. Lennard-Jones
interactions with a dissociation energy of 𝜀 = 12.0 kJ/mol were added based on structure-based
contacts derived from an overlap and contact of structural units-criterion. Contacts up to a
distance of 1.1 nm were taken into account27 resulting in 331 Lennard-Jones interactions. The
metal ions Cu(I) and Zn(II) are represented by tiny Q0 beads with a charge of +1. Although the
formal charge of the Zn metal is +2, in SOD1 (and other metalloenzymes) this charge is spread
between the Zn and its coordination shell, covalently bonded to the metal (both in CG and
atomistic models). Comparison to quantum mechanical calculations show that a lower charge
provides a more realistic model. Indeed, in the atomistic model, the charge on the Zn(II), derived
from RESP charges on an isolated model complex, is not +2, but around +0.4, with the rest of the
positive charged spread in some of the other atoms around. In a CG model as MARTINI, parts
of the electrostatic interactions, like dipoles and quadruples, are implicitly included in the LJ
potential. Besides, beads of different sizes represent different number of non-hydrogen atoms,
from 4 (regular beads) to 3 (small beads) and 2 (tiny beads). So, the tiny Q0 bead is, in this case,
representing Zn and part of its 1st coordination shell, which includes a negatively charged aspartic
acid. We ended up with an effective charge of +1, which is not very different from the sum of the
charges in the atomistic model. Similar considerations were taken into account to attribute the
charge of Cu(I) as +1. The bonded parameters of the metals were optimized to maintain a stable
coordination environment and to reproduce the flexibility observed in the atomistic simulations.
The effect of the G93A mutation was taken into account by changing the bead of residue 93 from
a SP1 bead representing glycine to a P1 bead representing alanine. Note that this is the only
difference between the CG models of WT SOD1 and its G93A mutant.

All analysis of the CG simulations were performed for the whole 480 µs of all trajectories.
Statistical errors were estimated using the block average method. Visual inspection of the
trajectories were performed with VMD28. RMSD calculations were performed using a homemade
Fortran program, based in MDLovoFit code29. The alignment protocol of the backbone beads can
be described in four steps: (i) standard rigid-body alignment of the structures using all BB beads
as reference; (ii) calculation of the RMSD per residue for all BB beads; (iii) identification of the
residues with RMSD lower than 1 Å; (iv) a new rigid body alignment using only this subgroup of
residues that represents the most rigid parts of SOD1. Steps (iii) and (iv) were repeated until the
RMSD and the residues used in the rigid subgroup are converged. As standard reference for the
trajectory alignment, we used the average structure obtained in the CG simulation of each system.
To calculate the water-active site contacts shown in Figure 2 of the manuscript and Figure S6B,
we employed the Gromacs tool gmx select to select all CG water beads within a distance cutoff
1.1 nm of the active site. The active site was defined as the two metal ions Zn(II) and Cu(I) as
well as their coordinating amino acids, residues 46, 48, 63, 71, 80, 83, and 120. The number of
water beads within the cutoff at each time step yield the actual water-active site contacts. The
free energies were obtained based on the population density along the 2D space described by
the RMSD of EL in relation to average structure of the whole data and the water-active site
contacts.

The distance distributions between all backbone beads of SOD1 were calculated using the
Gromacs tool gmx distance with a bin width of 0.01 nm. Examples of the distributions are shown
in Figure S7, panel A and B. To visualize the difference in the distributions of the WT and the

S2. Additional analyses

S2.1. Flexibility of EL loop in atomistic simulations.

WT simulations showed 2 trajectories with opening events of EL, which are indicated by RMSD
higher than 3.5 Å. In one case (WT-1), EL starts to open around 600 ns, with the full open state
(average RMSD of 6.0 Å) achieved in 800 ns. At the end of this simulation, EL come back to
intermediate open state, with RMSD around 3.5 Å. In the second simulation (WT-2), EL only
partially opens around 900 ns, staying in this state for around 50 ns. In the case of G93A, only
one MD simulation showed the opening of EL during the trajectory (G93A-3). In this case, EL
completely open in 500 ns (RMSD around 6.0 Å), without any stable intermediate open state been
formed in the whole simulation. EL stays open until the end of the 1 𝜇s. A comparison between
both systems does not indicate if WT or G93A show higher tendency to open EL and expose the
catalytic site to water molecules. While WT show two opening events, G93A definitely stay for
more time in the completely open state. As we highlighted in the main manuscript, the atomistic
simulation data is insufficient to judge whether there is an altered tendency for loop opening in
G93A SOD1.

S2.3. On the significance of the QM/MM energy values.

The QM/MM energies reported are obtained from four averages of 500 energies each, which are,
themselves sampled evenly from 5 ps runs (5000 single-points, see section S1.2). As the values
come separate averages, and are later combined, as opposed from an averaged combination of
values, it is difficult to obtain a single variance or equivalent value that would allow us to estimate
the reliability of the final differences. We can, though, compare the energy histograms for the pairs
of values that correspond to systems with the same number of atoms (i.e. the pair with Zn(II) and,
separately, the pair without Zn(II)). These histograms are shown in Figure S7. The histograms
show a clear separation between the different samples, indicating that the energy difference
reported is significant. I should be noted that, even though the histograms show a more negative
energy for the two EL-open conformations, EL opening is a relatively large conformational
change, and it would be incorrect to estimate it with a small sample like we employ here. Instead,
a method such as Umbrella Sampling, Metadynamics, or similar should be employed. The
instantaneous dissociation of Zn(II) is a local change, so it can be studied with a moderate
sampling as done here.
